The Anorak Story

Long before technical fabrics, Arctic peoples made hooded pullover jackets that shrugged off wind and snow. The anorak still carries their design.

01.1

Born in the Arctic

The word anorak comes from Greenlandic, and the garment itself was developed by Inuit and other Arctic peoples. Made from animal skins, these hooded pullovers were sometimes coated with fish oil to shed water, and were worn for hunting in kayaks.

01.2

A pullover, not a coat

The defining feature of an anorak is that it pulls on over the head rather than opening all the way down the front. With fewer openings there are fewer places for wind and water to get in, and the jacket can be lighter and simpler.

01.3

Into the mountains

Explorers and mountaineers adopted the design in the twentieth century.

  • Tightly woven cotton replaced skins.
  • Large chest pockets held maps and gloves.
  • Drawcord hems and hoods sealed out wind.

01.4

Synthetic shells

Nylon and later waterproof, breathable membranes made anoraks lighter, more weatherproof and easier to pack. Many modern anoraks fold into their own front pocket, making them a favourite for walking, cycling and travel.

01.5

The anorak today

Today’s anoraks range from rugged mountain shells to light city layers. Most share the same recipe: a half or quarter zip, a big hood, a front kangaroo pocket and a hem you can cinch. It is a design that has hardly needed to change.
